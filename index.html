// 01 — the story

Building options.

The short version of how I got here, one commit at a time.

00 2020 · age 11 main

Behind the counter.

In 2020 the pandemic hit our family's gift shop hard. With my mom taking a job elsewhere and my dad running the store alone, my afternoons moved from home to the shop — every single day after school.

I helped because my family needed it, and I'd do it again. But it also showed me something uncomfortable: even if I had wanted to say no, there was nothing else I could have said yes to.

So I started building alternatives.
01 10th grade r&d-team

More curious than confident.

In 10th grade I signed up for my school's R&D team. I wanted to build things far more than I wanted anyone watching me do it — and that's where my first real project came from.

02 2024 · TÜBİTAK 4006 first-presentation

Taking the mic.

The TÜBİTAK 4006 Science Fair was the first time my work had a real audience: visitors from all over the city. For a while, I stood back and let my teammate do the talking.

Then I thought about the kid behind the shop counter and stepped in. When the district's director of national education came by, I invited him over and gave him the demo myself, without a script.

The shy kid gave the demo.
at the fairPresented technical demonstrations of multiple personal robotics and system software projects.

04 the offer 2 options

Solo, by choice.

Then my teacher brought me a new challenge: another international robotics competition — except this time, I'd have to build the robot entirely on my own.

Turning it down would have been easy, and nobody would have blamed me. For once, saying no was a real choice. I took the harder path anyway, because resting on what I'd already won felt like the surest way to stop growing.

Same yes as at eleven.
Different reason.

05 2026 · 18th International MEB Robot Competition autonomous-car

The crossroads bug.

I entered the Fully Autonomous Car category, my first time working on autonomous systems. A trophy would have been nice; learning how to make a car drive itself was the real goal.

autonomous-car — build.log
$ ./autonomous-car --track test [FAIL] intersection read as off-road → car drives into a wall [ .. ] 7 days of debugging · ideas from teachers, friends, family [ .. ] seriously considered shelving the project [ OK ] wrote a new vision-debugger algorithm → intersections detected [ OK ] ~3 months of iteration → race-ready [FAIL] race day: lighting far brighter than in testing → no response [ OK ] patched after the test window closed · too late to score [ OK ] result: no trophy · lessons kept

The road-detection logic I started with simply couldn't handle intersections, so I rebuilt it. Race day exposed the next weak spot — light. No trophy this time, but plenty of lessons a win wouldn't have taught me.

Under the hood

Designing a Rust-based PID controller and attempting to integrate a Hailo-8 AI accelerator (YOLOv8) via PyO3 on a Raspberry Pi 5 served as an invaluable, hands-on learning experience in hardware integration.
